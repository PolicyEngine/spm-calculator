Calculating and projecting Supplemental Poverty Measure thresholds

Public consumption data, rolling forecasts, and a pre-committed 2025 validation

Author
Affiliation

Max Ghenis

PolicyEngine

Published

November 11, 2026

Abstract

The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) estimates Supplemental Poverty Measure (SPM) thresholds from a rolling five-year Consumer Expenditure Survey window. BLS updates the thresholds for spending on basic needs and publishes them in the following year. Analysts must therefore estimate unpublished thresholds to project poverty. We reconstruct thresholds approximately from public microdata and compare four projection rules. For 2020–2025, we obtain mean absolute errors across housing tenures of 2.29 percent with the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U), 1.65 percent with a composite price index, 0.51 percent with replicated consumption growth, and 0.90 percent with an equal blend of the latter two. We use revised data and overlapping estimation windows in these retrospective comparisons. We also evaluate a pre-committed 2025 nowcast against subsequently published thresholds. The blend yields a mean absolute error of 1.17 percent, compared with 2.58 percent for CPI-U adjustment and 0.75 percent for consumption growth alone. We obtain the largest error for renters. Beyond available microdata, we project expenditure and rent records through 2035 under explicit assumptions about prices and real spending. We also add home internet spending from the CE’s item-level records and estimate that it raises replicated thresholds by 0.3 to 4.0 percent. We then impute the five in-kind benefits BLS adds to spending. We estimate that they raise thresholds by a further 0.5 to 3.5 percent and change 2022–2025 growth by −1.62 to −0.19 points per tenure with the CE’s spending summaries, each change within one standard error of zero. From BLS’s published shares, we compute that its imputed benefits supply 4.9 to 12.9 percent of its thresholds’ growth beyond CPI-U over those years, and that its price index and out-of-pocket spending supply the rest. We give each benefit its part of the 1.2 multiplier in BLS’s formula; by the share rows BLS publishes for them, with the Other share kept separate, the benefits supply 3.5 to 11.8 percent. We evaluate prospective accuracy for 2025 only.

1 Introduction

The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) estimates national Supplemental Poverty Measure (SPM) thresholds from Consumer Expenditure (CE) Interview Survey spending on food, clothing, shelter, utilities, telephone, and internet. BLS varies the thresholds by housing tenure. The U.S. Census Bureau adjusts them for family composition and local housing costs (Interagency Technical Working Group on Developing a Supplemental Poverty Measure 2010; Garner and Munoz 2021). By updating basic-needs standards with consumption, the agencies follow the National Academy of Sciences proposal (Citro and Michael 1995).

BLS publishes thresholds in the following year. It published the 2024 thresholds in September 2025 and the 2025 thresholds on August 24, 2026 (U.S. Census Bureau 2025c; U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026c). Analysts projecting poverty before publication must estimate thresholds and SPM resources. Adjusting the last published threshold for inflation captures price changes. BLS also re-estimates the expenditure distribution when it calculates the threshold.

Urban Institute projections for 2021 adjusted the 2019 thresholds for projected inflation (Wheaton et al. 2021). Columbia’s monthly poverty framework initially carried the latest published thresholds forward, identifying the need for more timely consumption data to project new ones (Parolin et al. 2022). Researchers also use price adjustment to hold anchored-SPM thresholds fixed in real terms and compare resources and policy across years (Wimer et al. 2016; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine 2019). Creamer (2026b) proposes thresholds that update the standard of living once a decade and adjust it for prices with CPI-U in between. An anchored standard measures resource changes against a fixed real threshold; predicting the thresholds BLS will publish requires estimating changes in that standard.

We compare four rules for projecting BLS thresholds using an approximate reconstruction from public CE files. We reconstruct 2019–2025 thresholds, test sample sensitivities, and evaluate one-year projections for 2020–2025. We also evaluate a pre-committed 2025 nowcast against the thresholds BLS subsequently published.

Beyond available microdata, we project future expenditure and rental records and advance both five-year survey windows. These conditional forecasts use the CE and American Community Survey (ACS), with assumptions about prices, real expenditures, and rents.

2 Calculating the thresholds

2.1 National reference thresholds

BLS estimates a national threshold for a two-adult, two-child reference unit in each of three housing tenures: owners with a mortgage, owners without a mortgage, and renters. Census applies the thresholds to SPM resource units after adjusting for composition and local housing costs, and computes poverty status from the corresponding resources (Interagency Technical Working Group on Developing a Supplemental Poverty Measure 2010; Garner and Munoz 2021).

Under the methodology approved by the SPM Interagency Technical Working Group in September 2020, target year \(T\) uses five years of CE Interview collection quarters, \((T-5)\)Q2 through \(T\)Q1 (Burns and Fox 2021; U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026g). For consumer units with children, BLS constructs spending on food, clothing, shelter, utilities, telephone, and internet (FCSUti), including owners’ mortgage-principal payments and imputed in-kind benefits. The latter include broadband subsidies, energy assistance, school lunches, rental assistance, and the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children (WIC). BLS normalizes spending to the reference family using the three-parameter equivalence scale (Betson 1996; Garner and Munoz 2021) and updates it to target-year dollars with a composite FCSUti price index (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026g).

Let \(E\) denote the pooled, survey-weighted 47th–53rd percentile band of reference-family FCSUti expenditures across all housing tenures. Let \(SU\) denote shelter and utilities excluding telephone. For housing tenure \(h\), BLS calculates the national threshold as

\[ B_{T,h}=s\left(1.2\,\overline{FCSUti}_{E} -\overline{SU}_{E}+\overline{SU}_{E,h}\right). \]

The pooled expenditure band defines the basic-needs amount. The 1.2 multiplier allows for other basic goods and services, and replacing the pooled housing mean with the tenure-specific mean differentiates the three thresholds. In July 2026, BLS reissued its 2019–2024 thresholds after correcting its computer code (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026f), and Creamer (2026a) describes the correction as a fix to the imputation of in-kind benefits. The corrected series sets \(s\), the share of median expenditures, at 0.82, where the earlier series used 0.83 (Creamer 2026a).

We benchmark against the corrected BLS 2005–2024 workbook issued in July 2026 and the published 2025 values. In particular, the 2019–2024 comparisons use this July 2026 vintage. The historical comparisons therefore use information unavailable in the target years (Croushore and Stark 2001).

2.2 Applying a threshold to an SPM unit

Census scales each tenure’s reference threshold to the unit’s composition. Let \(q(A,C)\) denote that scale for \(A\) adults and \(C\) children, normalized to one for two adults and two children. For integer counts \(A\geq1\) and \(C\geq0\), write \(q(A,C)=e(A,C)/3^{0.7}\), where

\[ e(A,C)= \begin{cases} 1, & A=1,\ C=0,\\ 1.41, & A=2,\ C=0,\\ (1+0.8+0.5(C-1))^{0.7}, & A=1,\ C\geq1,\\ (A+0.5C)^{0.7}, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

The two-adult, no-child case uses Census’s stated constant 1.41 (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-b). Census assigns adults and children to SPM composition roles using its resource-unit definition. Age counts alone do not determine those roles. For example, one adult with two children has \(q(1,2)=2.3^{0.7}/3^{0.7}\), while \(q(2,2)=1\). Geographic adjustment applies to the housing portion. Writing that portion as share \(a_{T,h}\) and the local relative housing-cost index as \(g\), the calculation is

\[ B_{T,h}^{\mathrm{unit}} =B_{T,h}\,q(A,C)\left[(1-a_{T,h})+a_{T,h}g\right]. \]

The bracketed expression multiplies the whole threshold; the relative housing-cost index \(g\) adjusts only the housing portion. Resources below the resulting threshold imply SPM poverty; equality does not. Population models must supply SPM membership, composition roles, and resources consistently with Census definitions.

Both components in Table 1 use published 2025 values (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026c, 2026l). Geographic indices follow a separate estimation and publication schedule. A local threshold that uses a modeled geographic index combines official and modeled inputs. Local estimation areas comprise metropolitan statistical areas and Census’s residual metropolitan and nonmetropolitan groups (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-b). Counties identify the applicable estimation area. Section 7 describes the annual geographic projection.

3 Replication from public consumption data

3.1 Expenditure construction and estimation sample

We read public CE Interview family-level FMLI files for each target year’s five-year window. Each record divides one three-month recall period between previous-quarter and current-quarter expenditure fields. We sum the fields and multiply by four to annualize spending. We retain telephone spending in FCSUti and exclude it from the narrower shelter-utilities measure. We include mortgage principal from the FMLI outlay fields (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026e, 2026g).

Newer CE records combine grocery spending in one summary. We allocate 80 percent of that summary to food; BLS specifies a narrower item-code allocation in its errata. We omit home-internet spending, which the public FMLI summaries lack, and add it in Section 4 from the CE’s item-level records, along with imputed in-kind benefits.

We normalize each consumer unit’s expenditures to the two-adult, two-child reference family before estimating the expenditure band and housing means. We use CE interview survey weights and an inclusive 47th–53rd percentile band from a midpoint weighted cumulative distribution. We adjust each interview’s spending with the annual index for its collection year. BLS adjusts each quarter’s spending to threshold-year dollars with an index it produces as an annual average (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026g, 2026c), and we have not established which year’s index it applies to each interview quarter. For each target year, we derive expenditure-deflation weights from the selected CE sample before selecting the percentile band. We use separate, fixed weights for the price comparator in Section 5. We have not verified the percentile calculation against BLS’s code.

Independent minors can form CE consumer units, so age counts alone cannot establish the SPM roles needed for equivalence scaling. We exclude these unresolved records from the baseline sample. To test sensitivity, we assign one adult and retain the reported child count. This nonofficial recode leaves membership unresolved. We also exclude units that occupy their homes without paying cash rent (tenure code 5) and units in student housing (code 6), as BLS does (Schild 2023). BLS also drops owners who do not report whether they hold a mortgage (code 3); we would assign them to owners with a mortgage, and these windows contain none. We have not established how BLS treats independent minors.

3.2 Replicated levels and sample sensitivities

We reconstruct seven target years, 2019–2025. Table 2 reports signed deviations from the corrected published thresholds. Differences vary by tenure and year, including positive deviations for owners without mortgages in later windows. The comparison measures the combined discrepancy; it does not identify the contribution of any particular omitted component or sample rule.

We obtain a maximum threshold change of 1.74 percent when we include tenure codes 5 and 6 as renters (Table 3). We observe no code-3 records, so assigning that code to owners without a mortgage changes no estimates. We cannot infer its effect in a sample that contains those records. We count consumer-unit interviews in the weighted shares, including repeated interviews.

We test sensitivity to youth and tenure treatment. The assignment of interviews to price years, the food allocation, omitted home internet and in-kind benefits (Section 4), and percentile conventions can also affect the estimates. We estimate standard errors with the CE’s replicate weights for the replication with home internet added (Section 4.6). They run 0.88 to 1.27 times BLS’s published ones for owners with mortgages, 0.91 to 1.46 times for owners without mortgages, and 0.62 to 1.04 times for renters. Garner (2010) estimated the standard errors of earlier BLS thresholds by balanced repeated replication with the CE’s 44 replicate weights, as we do, and Garner and Gudrais (2018) base theirs on the same 44 replicates. BLS’s methods page and threshold workbooks do not say how its current replicates treat the percentile band or the imputed benefits, so the two calculations can still differ. We use many of the same CE interviews as BLS, so neither set of standard errors measures the sampling error in the gap between our thresholds and BLS’s.

4 Home internet and in-kind benefits

Before it estimates the thresholds, BLS adds the value of five in-kind benefits to each consumer unit’s out-of-pocket FCSUti: school lunches, WIC, payments from the Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP), broadband subsidies, and rental assistance (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026g). It adds no benefits from the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP). Because SNAP benefits arrive by electronic benefit transfer (EBT), BLS assumes that households use them like cash or credit to buy food, so reported food spending already includes them (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026g). For the same reason, BLS writes that it adds WIC only where a state has not fully implemented WIC EBT (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026g). BLS describes its current imputation models briefly on its methods page (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026g), and Garner and Gudrais (2018) document an earlier version in more detail. BLS has not published its code, so we implement the page’s description in the companion calculator (PolicyEngine 2026a). We rerun the 2019–2025 replication three ways: as in Section 3, with home internet added, and with home internet and the five benefits. The first run reproduces the thresholds behind Table 2 exactly. We omit home internet and the benefits from the projections, the 2025 nowcast, and the conditional forecasts in later sections.

4.1 Home internet

BLS records each unit’s spending by item code in the CE’s item-level MTBI files (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026e). For each unit, we sum its MTBI records under the CE’s Universal Classification Code (UCC) 690114, computer information services, from the same quarter’s file as its FMLI record. We multiply that three-month total by four, as we do for its other spending. We add the result to FCSUti but not to shelter and utilities. We price it with the CPI-U index for internet services and electronic information providers (CUUR0000SEEE03). We add that index to the composite CPI at home internet’s weighted share of FCSUti spending among units with children and scale the other components’ weights down in proportion. We add home internet before we estimate the thresholds, so it can change which units fall in the percentile band. We have not established which item codes BLS counts as home internet.

In our band with home internet and the benefits, we estimate home internet spending of $543 to $869 per unit across the 2019–2025 thresholds, against $547 to $878 in BLS’s band (Section 4.4). Across those years, our estimate differs from BLS’s by −1.1 to +3.2 percent. In its formula, BLS adds 0.2 times the band’s mean FCSUti for other basic goods and services, and it publishes that term’s share of each threshold as a separate Other share. With that share kept separate, home internet supplies 1.54 to 2.12 percent of each BLS threshold, and we estimate that it supplies 1.49 to 2.04 percent of ours with home internet and the benefits. When we give it its part of the Other share, it supplies 1.85 to 2.55 percent of BLS’s thresholds and, we estimate, 1.78 to 2.44 percent of ours.

4.2 Imputation models

As BLS describes, we fit weighted logistic regressions on households in the Current Population Survey Annual Social and Economic Supplement (CPS ASEC) and use them to assign participation in free or reduced-price school lunch, WIC, LIHEAP, and broadband subsidies to CE consumer units (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026g; U.S. Census Bureau 2025b). We assign each CE interview to its collection year, and first-quarter interviews to the year before. For each of those reference years, we use donors from the following year’s CPS ASEC. For all four programs, we use covariates that both surveys measure: income relative to the official poverty threshold; receipt of SNAP, public assistance, Supplemental Security Income, and housing assistance; tenure; and household size. We also use counts of children under 5, children aged 5–18, members 65 and older, and earners, along with the reference person’s age, sex, race, Hispanic origin, and education. We add state or region indicators where the CE identifies them. We scale the survey weights to average one and add a ridge penalty of one to every slope, in these models and in the amount and rent regressions below. Garner and Gudrais (2018) list the covariates of BLS’s earlier models. Their list includes marital status, employment status, disability, single parenthood, Medicaid, and urban residence, which ours omits, and they pool five years of CPS ASEC households with state fixed effects, where we fit each reference year separately. The school lunch model covers households with a member aged 5–18. The WIC model covers the CPS ASEC WIC universe as BLS defines it: households with a woman aged 15 to 45, or with a child under 5 and a woman aged 15 or older (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026g). For the 2025 thresholds, as BLS does, we also let the WIC model assign participation to CE units that have a child under 5 but no woman aged 15 or older (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026g). We assign every interview in our 2025 window to reference year 2020 or later, and we add no WIC from reference year 2020 on, so these units take none.

We assign LIHEAP and broadband amounts by predictive mean matching. We run a weighted regression of log amounts on the covariates among CPS ASEC recipients, and each imputed CE recipient takes the amount of one of the five recipients nearest it in predicted value. Households report their LIHEAP amounts. For broadband, households report the months they received a subsidy, and Census values each month at the program’s monthly benefit for their area, from 2021 until the Affordable Connectivity Program ended in June 2024 (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-b). BLS includes imputed broadband subsidies in its 2023–2025 thresholds, from data that start in 2022 (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026j). We impute them from reference year 2022 too, so they first enter our 2023 thresholds.

BLS assigns each imputed participant a free or reduced-price lunch from the unit’s before-tax income and values it with amounts it receives from Census staff (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026g). We do not have those amounts. From reference year 2018, we use the value Census assigns each SPM unit, which every public CPS ASEC person file carries from the 2019 file onward (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-b). In those years, each imputed participant receives the value Census assigns a participating CPS ASEC household in its cell. We form cells by state, SNAP receipt, income group, and number of school-age children, top-coded at four, and scale the value to the unit’s children aged 5–18. We split the income groups at 150 percent of the official poverty line. When a cell has fewer than five donors, or the CE withholds the unit’s state, we widen it in steps: from state to region, then across numbers of children, then across regions, and last to all donors. Census applies its pandemic-year methods in those values, and BLS says it follows them (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026g; U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-b). For reference years 2014–2017, Census publishes SPM-unit values in research files, and for 2017 also in the 2018 CPS ASEC Bridge File, while the CPS ASEC family records carry its traditional valuation, F_MV_SL (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-b). We read none of these. For those years, we value each CPS ASEC donor’s reported lunches at USDA’s reimbursement and donated-food rates over 179 school days. We use the free rate below 150 percent of the poverty line and otherwise choose the free or reduced-price rate with equal probability, as BLS does for CE units (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026g). We then carry those donor values to CE units through the same cells.

Following Census (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-b), we count every child under 5 in each participating unit, plus one mother if the unit has a child aged 0 or 1. We count that mother only when the unit has a woman aged 15 or older, so in the units we add to the WIC universe for 2025, we count the children alone. If the unit has no child under 5, we count one pregnant woman. Where the CE reports the unit’s state, we value each participant’s WIC at twelve times the average monthly WIC food cost per person in that state, for the fiscal year that matches the unit’s reference year. The Census Bureau’s SPM team compiled these state averages from the WIC program data of USDA’s Food and Nutrition Service (FNS) and provided them to us. We publish their table as received in the calculator’s repository (PolicyEngine 2026d). Census values WIC with state averages (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-b), and BLS assigns the WIC values Census staff supply (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026g). Census valued WIC at USDA’s national average until its September 2021 report, when it adopted state averages and recomputed its 2019 estimates with them (Burns and Fox 2021). We apply the state averages in every reference year in which we add WIC, 2014 to 2019.

We add no WIC from reference year 2020, the year of the statutory deadline. Congress required every WIC state agency to implement EBT throughout the state by October 1, 2020, unless the Secretary of Agriculture exempted it (U.S. Congress 2010). We assign interviews from April 2020 to March 2021 to reference year 2020, and their reference periods center on mid-February 2020 to mid-January 2021. So we also drop WIC for reference periods before the deadline, including in six states whose WIC agencies implemented EBT statewide between June and September 2020, from Hawaii to Illinois (U.S. Department of Agriculture, Food and Nutrition Service 2022). The WIC agencies of six more states and the District of Columbia did so after the deadline, from Utah in November 2020 to Georgia in October 2022 (U.S. Department of Agriculture, Food and Nutrition Service 2022). For earlier reference years, we take each state’s statewide date from FNS and add no WIC when the unit’s state had fully implemented WIC EBT by the midpoint of its three-month reference period. When the CE withholds a unit’s state, we draw that status with probability equal to the weighted share of known-state units in the same region, collection year, and collection quarter whose states had done so. Where that cell has no known-state unit, as when the CE also withholds the region, we count the state as having done so and add no WIC. If a unit with a withheld state takes WIC, we value each participant’s WIC at twelve times a weighted mean monthly food cost per person. We average over the cell’s known-state units whose states had not fully implemented WIC EBT, weighting each unit by its survey weight and taking its state’s cost for the fiscal year that matches the cell’s reference year.

We assign every interview in our 2025 window to reference year 2020 or later, so our 2025 thresholds include no WIC. If we instead apply BLS’s rule state by state in every reference year, we add WIC in the 12 states and the District whose statewide dates fall in June 2020 or later, each until its date. Some units in the 2025 window then take WIC. We estimate that mean WIC per CE unit with children rises by $2.07 to $3.45 in the windows behind the 2021–2025 thresholds. As the added WIC raises spending in the band and changes which units fall in it, we estimate that those thresholds move by −$9 to +$19 (−0.03 to +0.06 percent). We estimate a change of 0.03 points or less in 2022–2025 growth.

For rental assistance, BLS fits a limited dependent variable model that treats assisted renters’ rents as censored from above, and it values the benefit as imputed market rent less rent paid (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026g). Garner and Gudrais (2018) document an earlier two-step model: a logistic regression for paying market rent, then, among the renters who do, a regression of log rent on building characteristics and the first step’s Mills ratio, with state and year effects. They predict a market rent for each assisted unit and count a subsidy where that rent exceeds the rent paid. We take each unit’s monthly rent paid as one third of its three-month rent total in the CE’s RNT file, which matches its spending on rent of the dwelling (UCC 210110). In the 2015 CE Interview questionnaire, BLS asks for each month’s rent (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2015), but it releases only the three-month total in the public RNT file (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026e). We fit a weighted Tobit model of log monthly rent on CE renters in each reference year, and treat the rents of units that report government-reduced rent or public housing as censored from above at the rent they pay. We condition on bedrooms, bathrooms, building type, urbanicity, population size, state or region, and interview quarter. We leave unassisted renters who pay under $50 a month, and assisted renters who report no rent, out of the fit. We draw a market rent from the fitted distribution above each assisted unit’s paid rent and value its benefit at twelve times the difference, so every assisted unit receives a positive benefit.

We draw five implicates, each with a fresh draw of every model’s parameters from their estimated sampling distribution (Rubin 1987), so that we can measure imputation uncertainty from the spread across implicates. We add every benefit to FCSUti and add LIHEAP and rental assistance to shelter and utilities as well. We carry each unit’s benefits to threshold-year dollars with the price factor we apply to its out-of-pocket spending.

4.3 Threshold levels

We estimate that home internet raises the replicated thresholds by 1.66 to 2.14 percent for owners with mortgages, 0.34 to 4.00 percent for owners without mortgages, and 1.53 to 2.38 percent for renters. When we then impute the benefits, we estimate that they raise the thresholds by a further 0.5 to 3.5 percent. With both, we estimate thresholds that exceed BLS’s for every tenure in every year, by 2.18 to 7.07 percent (Table 4). We read the CE’s shelter, utilities, and food summaries, which include rent and meals received as pay, other lodging such as vacation homes and lodging on out-of-town trips (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026d), and utilities for vacation homes (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026e). Garner and Munoz (2021) restrict shelter to the primary residence and leave out rent and meals received as pay. They count owners’ utilities for the primary residence only, and renters’ utilities for any property but a rented vacation home. When we apply only these Garner–Munoz restrictions, we remove those amounts and the vacation-home mortgage principal we add to shelter. We then estimate that the replication without home internet or benefits trails BLS by 1.98 to 4.20 percent. With home internet, we estimate thresholds that differ from BLS’s by −2.13 to +0.90 percent, and the mean absolute deviation falls from 3.14 to 1.11 percent. When we also add the imputed benefits, we estimate thresholds that exceed BLS’s for every tenure in every year, by 0.75 to 3.63 percent, and the mean absolute deviation rises to 1.76 percent.

4.4 BLS’s imputed amounts

BLS computes each component’s share of each threshold from band means and publishes the shares, together with each tenure’s share of the band (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026l, 2026c). The 2019–2024 shares reproduce the thresholds BLS reissued in July 2026. Let \(C\) sum the components common to every tenure and \(P_h\) denote tenure \(h\)’s share of the band, so that \(\overline{FCSUti}_E=\overline{C}_E+\sum_h P_h\,\overline{SU}_{E,h}\). Each threshold then satisfies

\[ B_{T,h}/s=\overline{C}_E+\overline{SU}_{E,h}+0.2\,\overline{FCSUti}_E, \]

and a component’s share times \(B_{T,h}/s\) gives its band mean in dollars. BLS publishes the share of the last term, \(0.2\,\overline{FCSUti}_E\), as a separate Other share. We give each component its part of it: a component \(c\) common to every tenure puts the term \(1.2\,s\,\overline{c}_E\) in \(B_{T,h}\), and a shelter or utility component puts \(s\,(\overline{c}_{E,h}+0.2\,\overline{c}_E)\), so the terms sum to the threshold. Besides its broadband subsidy row, BLS publishes two internet rows. We use the one that gives equal dollars across tenures: “OOP Internet” through 2024 and “Total Internet Share” in 2025. We treat that row as including broadband subsidies and subtract them to recover home internet spending. With the Other share kept separate, we read BLS’s home internet share for a tenure as that row less the tenure’s own broadband row. With these readings, BLS’s published shares, thresholds, and tenure shares satisfy both identities within $0.00001 in every year from 2019 to 2025. When we keep the Other share separate, the terms we compute from BLS’s band means match its published share for Other and for each component, including home internet, within the same bound.

Between the 2022 and 2025 thresholds, BLS’s imputed benefits rose from $855 to $1,257 per unit in its band, and we estimate that ours rose from $1,029 to $1,170, a rise of $141 with a standard error of $189 (Table 5). Of the $260 difference between the two rises, rental assistance supplies $174 and school lunch $92, and the other three benefits narrow it by $6. From the 2019 to the 2022 thresholds, BLS’s rental assistance per renter rose 17 percent, and we estimate that ours rose 14 percent (Table 6). For the 2019–2023 thresholds, we estimate rental assistance per renter 58 to 87 percent above BLS’s, $1,347 against $738 in 2022. BLS’s amount then rose to $940 in 2024 and $1,243 in 2025, where we estimate $1,448. From 2022 to 2025, BLS’s amount rose by $505, and we estimate that ours rose by $101, with a standard error of $452. BLS draws on many of the same CE interviews, so that standard error does not measure the sampling error in the gap between the two rises. We have not established what produces the level gap or what raised BLS’s amount in 2024 and 2025 (Section 4.6).

4.5 Threshold growth, 2022–2025

From 2022 to 2025, CPI-U rose 10.01 percent, BLS’s FCSUti price index 14.19 percent, and BLS’s thresholds 20.63 to 22.15 percent (Table 7). Schild (2023) attributes 7.21 points of the 2021–2022 rise in the thresholds BLS first published to its FCSUti index, and 2.5 to 2.9 points to newer CE data and changes in BLS’s code for imputing in-kind benefits. We start our split from the 2022 thresholds BLS reissued in July 2026 and the shares from which we reproduce them. We use CPI-U’s official annual averages. BLS’s 2025 thresholds page uses a twelve-month 2025 average with October interpolated, 138.289 against the official 138.198 (2013 = 100) (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026c), which puts CPI-U’s 2022–2025 growth at 10.08 percent. We estimate that home internet changes the replication’s growth by +0.01 points for owners with mortgages, +4.33 for owners without mortgages, and −0.54 for renters. With home internet added, we estimate that the imputed benefits change growth by −0.19, −1.62, and −0.26 points, each within one standard error of zero (Section 4.6). When we deflate with BLS’s FCSUti index instead of the replication’s composite CPI, we estimate that growth with home internet changes by +0.97, −0.12, and +1.29 points. Under the Garner–Munoz restrictions and BLS’s index, we estimate growth with home internet and the benefits that differs from BLS’s by +0.54, −0.52, and −1.13 points. With the same restrictions and the replication’s composite CPI, we estimate that the benefits change growth by +0.41, −4.49, and −0.02 points.

BLS’s shares, its FCSUti index, and CPI-U split each threshold’s growth beyond CPI-U into three parts (Table 8). For owners with mortgages, BLS’s threshold outgrew CPI-U by 11.61 points of its 2022 level. The FCSUti index outgrew CPI-U by 4.18 points, which, on the threshold’s out-of-pocket terms, supplies 4.11 points. Growth in the imputed benefits’ terms beyond CPI-U supplies 0.57 points, and growth in out-of-pocket spending beyond BLS’s index supplies the remaining 6.92. The three parts supply 4.10, 0.81, and 5.72 of 10.63 points for owners without mortgages, and 4.04, 1.57, and 6.54 of 12.14 points for renters. Imputed benefits therefore supply 4.9, 7.6, and 12.9 percent of BLS’s growth beyond CPI-U, and we estimate 2.3, 2.5, and 1.5 percent for ours. In both series, we count home internet with out-of-pocket spending. We compute each share as \((I_{2025}-\pi I_{2022})/(B_{2025}-\pi B_{2022})\), where \(I\) sums the imputed benefits’ terms in threshold \(B\) and \(\pi\) denotes CPI-U’s growth factor. When we keep the Other share separate, as BLS publishes it, \(I\) sums the benefits’ five share rows times \(B\), and we count the Other share with out-of-pocket spending. The benefits then supply 3.5, 5.8, and 11.8 percent of BLS’s growth beyond CPI-U, and out-of-pocket spending and the Other share beyond the index supply 60.9, 55.4, and 54.8 percent. If we apply the index’s lead to the benefits as well, out-of-pocket spending keeps its part in either accounting. The benefits’ part falls to 4.3, 6.8, and 11.7 percent with their terms, and to 3.0, 5.2, and 10.7 percent with their rows. We take each year’s band means as BLS’s shares give them, so we leave out how the benefits change which units fall in the band. In our replication, we estimate that this re-ranking moves 2022–2025 growth by −0.28, −1.70, and +0.05 points. The out-of-pocket part combines real spending growth with changes in the sample as newer CE quarters replace older ones. With the composite CPI, we estimate that our renter growth with home internet and the benefits trails BLS’s by 2.58 points, and by 1.09 points when we remove the imputed terms from both series.

For renters from 2024 to 2025, where the 2025 nowcast missed most (Section 6.2), BLS’s threshold grew 6.33 percent. With its imputed benefits’ terms removed from both years, it grows 5.71 percent, 0.62 points less, and rental assistance alone accounts for 0.57 of those points. BLS’s rental assistance per renter rose from $940 to $1,243 between those thresholds, and we estimate that ours rose from $1,277 to $1,448. We estimate the retrospective replication’s renter growth at 4.85 percent without home internet or benefits, 4.58 percent with home internet, and 4.82 percent with both.

4.6 Uncertainty and limitations

We estimate sampling uncertainty by balanced repeated replication with the CE’s 44 replicate weights (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026k; PolicyEngine 2026b). Under each replicate weight, we rerun the whole threshold estimator, from the expenditure-deflation weights to the percentile band, with home internet and with both home internet and the imputed benefits. We hold each implicate’s imputed amounts fixed. Every replicate includes home internet, so we report no standard error for home internet’s change to growth. When we deflate with BLS’s FCSUti index instead of the replication’s composite CPI, we apply its published values in every replicate, so the price factors do not vary. We combine the replicate variance with the variance across implicates by Rubin’s rules (Rubin 1987). For the benefits’ change to 2022–2025 growth, we estimate standard errors of 0.67 points for owners with mortgages, 3.18 for owners without mortgages, and 0.82 for renters. Under either price index, every change lies within three-quarters of a standard error of zero. For the 2022–2025 rises in our band amounts, we estimate standard errors of $189 for all imputed benefits, $452 for rental assistance per renter, and $46 for school lunch. For the benefits’ lift to each year’s threshold with the composite CPI, we estimate standard errors of 0.27 to 0.57 points for owners with mortgages and renters, whose lifts exceed five standard errors in every year. For owners without mortgages, we estimate standard errors of 1.01 to 2.21 points, and the 2024–2025 lifts each fall within 1.5 standard errors of zero.

For each of the benefits’ changes to 2022–2025 growth, we estimate that the spread across implicates accounts for 8 to 30 percent of the variance under Rubin’s rules. Because we draw fresh model parameters for each implicate, that spread includes part of the models’ estimation uncertainty. We estimate the parameter covariances without accounting for either survey’s strata or clusters. Since we fix each implicate’s imputed amounts, the replicate variance leaves out sampling variation in the rental model’s fit and in the CPS ASEC donor samples. BLS imputes missing CE income five times, and we use the mean of the five as each unit’s income, so our standard errors also leave out the spread across them. For most units interviewed in both 2015Q1 and 2015Q2, or in both 2016Q4 and 2017Q1, BLS leaves a different set of replicate weights blank in the two quarters. Replicates for the 2019–2021 thresholds, whose windows span at least one of those pairs, can then miss part of the correlation between a unit’s interviews.

We use one covariate set for the CPS-to-CE models across programs and years, and the CE and CPS ASEC measure income differently: the CE’s before-tax income, which we divide by the poverty threshold, includes SNAP benefits, and the CPS ASEC’s money income does not (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026e; U.S. Census Bureau 2025b). We take reported rents as exact in the rental model. Garner and Gudrais (2018) pool the window’s years, condition their first step on program participation and household characteristics, and assign each assisted unit its predicted rent; we fit each reference year separately on unit characteristics and draw each market rent. The public files do not identify rent-controlled units, and our market-rent sample keeps units that receive rent as pay. We test two of these choices on reference year 2022, for which we estimate rental assistance of $10,809 per assisted renter across the CE. A unit that paid rent for only part of the three months shows a low monthly rent. If we replace a renter’s rent paid with the rent it reports at its next interview, wherever the rent paid is 25 to 75 percent of that later rent, we estimate that the amount rises 0.8 percent. Of the 6,159 renters, 3,120 report rent at a next interview, and we replace the rent of 156 of those, including 29 assisted renters. If we instead give each assisted unit its predicted market rent, as Garner and Gudrais (2018) do, and set negative benefits to zero, we estimate that the amount falls 30 percent at the log model’s median and 16 percent at its mean. If we fit the same model to rent in dollars and use its linear prediction, we estimate that the amount falls 13 percent. For these three, we fix each model’s parameters at their point estimates, while for our base estimate of $10,809 we average five implicates and draw new parameters for each.

Our school lunch donors report free or reduced-price lunches, while Census also values paid lunches and, for respondents who report EBT benefits but no SNAP, summer Pandemic EBT (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-b; Shrider 2023). BLS says it values school lunches by the traditional method before and after the COVID-19 period (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026g), and we use Census’s SPM-unit values for every reference year from 2018; from reference year 2023, Census estimates those values with its fall 2022 method (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-b). Census accounts for Pandemic EBT in its 2020 and 2021 values and for summer Pandemic EBT in its 2022 values (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-b; Shrider 2023). States paid those benefits on EBT cards, so under BLS’s reasoning for SNAP, reported food spending may already include them. From BLS’s 2025 shares, we compute WIC of $1.80 per unit in its band, where we add none. With the Garner–Munoz restrictions, we depart from Garner and Munoz (2021) in three ways. We keep telephone out of utilities, as BLS now does (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026l), where Garner and Munoz (2021) count it as a utility. We keep spending under two codes for other fuels in vacation homes, which the FMLI summaries of vacation-home utilities leave out (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026e). We also keep owners’ spending under the CE’s rented-dwelling summary, which averages $52 to $655 per owner without a mortgage in the band, and renters’ spending under its owned-dwelling summary. BLS’s current code may differ from that paper.

BLS changed the CE Interview’s sections on owned and rental property and on utilities and fuels in April 2024 (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026h), and interviews from April 2024 on first enter the 2025 thresholds. In the utilities section, BLS reworded the questions on cable, internet, and residential and cellular phone service, asked for expenses including sales taxes and fees, and dropped follow-up questions on bundled phone, internet, and cable amounts (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026i). Before 2017, BLS could overwrite a reported combined bill for telephone, internet, and television with a donor’s amounts by hot-deck imputation. From 2017, it keeps the reported total and splits it by proportions it computes from a pool of donors (Hubener et al. 2018). The windows behind the 2019–2021 thresholds include interviews from before that change. We have not tested whether rents, housing assistance, utilities, or home internet shift at either change.

5 Projecting thresholds

5.1 Price and consumption growth rules

We test whether public consumption growth predicts published thresholds more accurately than price adjustment. Let \(\widetilde B_{T,h}\) be the replicated national threshold and \(P_T\) a price index. Starting from an official base \(B_{T-1,h}\), the combined rule is

\[ \widehat B_{T,h}=B_{T-1,h} \left[\lambda\frac{\widetilde B_{T,h}}{\widetilde B_{T-1,h}} +(1-\lambda)\frac{P_T}{P_{T-1}}\right]. \]

We compare All-Items CPI-U adjustment; adjustment by a static-weight composite matching the FCSUti components available in the data; the replication growth ratio alone; and an equal blend of replication growth and composite-price growth. The last three correspond to \(\lambda=0\), \(1\), and \(1/2\) when \(P\) is the composite index. We fix the blend weights at one half.

We construct the projection composite from five CPI-U components: food, apparel, shelter, fuels and utilities, and telephone services. We assign respective weights of \((0.30, 0.05, 0.45, 0.12, 0.04)/0.96\), with no separate home-internet component, to approximate the BLS deflator. For component \(i\)’s annual-average index \(P_{i,T}\), we calculate the six-year comparison’s price multiplier as \(\sum_i w_i(P_{i,T}/P_{i,T-1})\). We use the same normalized weights in the five-year benchmark and 2025 nowcast. We hold the benchmark’s index base at 2019 and rebase the nowcast to the prior year. We derive the separate expenditure-adjustment weights from each selected CE sample.

If replication differs from the official level by a stable proportional factor, that factor cancels between adjacent years. A persistent additive difference need not cancel, and changing omissions or sample effects can alter growth.

Census estimated that updating the 2023 SPM thresholds by CPI-U growth would produce a 2024 SPM rate of 12.4 percent, below the published 2024 rate (Creamer 2025). Census used the threshold and poverty estimates published for 2024 in that analysis.

5.2 Retrospective comparison, 2020–2025

We evaluate six one-year projections for 2020–2025 (Table 9). For every target year, we start each rule from the prior year’s revised published threshold. We hold the composite’s component weights fixed and rebase its price index to the prior year. We use the replication and price inputs recorded in September 2026. We do not reconstruct the data available at each historical forecast date.

Over these six years, the replication ratio has the lowest mean absolute error, followed by the blend, composite-price adjustment, and CPI-U adjustment. Errors vary across years. We use realized prices, which analysts could not observe before the target year ended. Adjacent CE windows share four of five years, and tenure estimates share observations. These shared observations make the errors dependent, and all six comparisons use outcomes known at implementation.

5.3 Five-year benchmark for the 2025 nowcast

We use a five-year benchmark covering 2020–2024 for the 2025 nowcast (Table 10). We use corrected thresholds and realized prices in this retrospective comparison. For this benchmark, we use sample rules and a fixed-2019-base price composite that differ from those in the six-year comparison. Appendix B identifies the implementation and inputs for each experiment.

CPI-U adjustment has a mean absolute error of 2.23 percent and understates threshold growth in four of five years, with a signed mean error of −1.9 percent. Composite-price adjustment reduces the mean absolute error to 1.57 percent, removing about thirty percent of the CPI-U error in this sample. The replication ratio scores 0.41 percent, with a signed mean of +0.1 percent and annual signed means ranging from +0.5 to −0.3 percent. The equal blend scores 0.76 percent; its annual signed means range from −0.1 to −1.4 percent. These ranges describe variation across the five observed years; they do not quantify future forecast uncertainty.

6 Pre-committed 2025 validation

6.1 The estimate before publication

We estimated the 2025 thresholds after the expenditure period ended and before BLS published the outcome, producing a nowcast (Giannone et al. 2008). We used the CE window from 2020Q2 through 2025Q1 and eleven months of 2025 CPI observations. BLS did not publish October 2025 CPI. We average the other eleven months following BLS’s documented treatment (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026b).

We amended the forecast on July 18, 2026 to correct the price composite. The forecast record links the August 7 archived page, tags, and timestamp proof that document both forecasts before BLS’s August 24 publication.

We retained the equal blend as the primary forecast after the correction ranked the replication ratio first.1

We committed to an equal blend that projected $41,036.34 for owners with mortgages, $34,135.99 for owners without mortgages, and $40,755.98 for renters. We apply replication and composite-price growth to the corrected 2024 thresholds specified in the forecast registration.

Replicated threshold growth ranged from 4.4 to 6.0 percent by tenure, against 3.2 percent composite-price growth. Their blend projected 3.8 to 4.6 percent growth, compared with 2.6 percent realized CPI-U growth. The nowcast price component chain-links at the prior year; the five-year backtest composite holds a fixed 2019 base. The two composites therefore apply the same component weights to different index bases.

6.2 Evaluation against published thresholds

BLS published the 2025 thresholds on August 24, 2026: $41,323 for owners with mortgages, $34,326 for owners without, and $41,701 for renters (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026c). The linked workbook carries full-precision values and standard errors of $327, $560, and $393. BLS reports growth of 4.40 to 6.33 percent by tenure over the corrected 2024 base. We use that base, fixed before publication, for every forecast evaluation.

The committed blend understates the three thresholds by 0.69, 0.55, and 2.27 percent, giving a mean absolute error of 1.17 percent. The signed mean error also equals −1.17 percent. The owner differences fall within one published BLS standard error; the renter difference reaches about 2.4 standard errors. BLS standard errors measure official sampling uncertainty, so they cannot bound forecast errors or test the model’s discrepancies for significance.

CPI-U adjustment understates all three thresholds by 1.70 to 3.47 percent, for a mean absolute error of 2.58 percent. The replication ratio alone scores 0.75 percent and has errors on both sides of zero; composite-price adjustment scores 2.02 percent. The replication ratio therefore ranks first in this validation year as well as in the five-year retrospective comparison. One year cannot establish whether any of these rankings will persist.

The largest miss occurs for renters. BLS reports renter growth of 6.33 percent against 5.33 and 4.40 percent for the two owner tenures. The blend projects renter growth of 3.92 percent, and replication alone 4.62 percent. If we take each year’s band as BLS’s shares give it and remove its imputed benefits’ terms from both years, that renter growth falls by 0.62 points, mostly because of rental assistance. We estimate that home internet changes the retrospective replication’s 2024–2025 renter growth by −0.27 points and that our imputed benefits then change it by +0.24 points (Section 4). Price treatment, sample classification, and percentile conventions can also affect growth, and the comparison does not isolate their contributions to the renter miss.

6.3 Validation of the price comparator

BLS publishes its FCSUti deflator growth alongside the thresholds, providing an additional comparison after publication. We compare BLS’s deflator with the five-year backtest’s fixed-2019-base composite. The 2025 nowcast uses a separate, prior-year-based composite. The fixed-base composite differs from the BLS index by at most 0.7 percentage point in each year from 2020 through 2025, with a mean absolute gap of 0.4 point and no consistent sign. The composite approximates the deflator over these six years.

For 2025, the BLS threshold page reports CPI-U growth of 2.70 percent, and the eleven-month annual average in the pinned CPI observations implies 2.63 percent. 7 Conditional forecasts beyond available microdata

7.1 Projecting expenditure records and rolling windows

For the retrospective comparisons, we observe the CE collection window for each target year. To forecast later thresholds, we supply the unobserved quarters and re-estimate each twenty-quarter window. We use CE records available on September 9, 2026, through 2025Q1. At that date, BLS scheduled the next annual CE microdata release for October 29, 2026 (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026a). For each later quarter, we reuse records from the latest observed quarter of the same season. We preserve their survey weights, composition, and identities, and multiply spending by cumulative price change and an assumed real-expenditure growth factor. We remove historical quarters as projected quarters enter and recalculate each threshold from the resulting sample.

BLS interrupted CE collection in October and November 2025. It adjusts its annual expenditure estimates for this interruption but does not apply those adjustments directly to the public-use files (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026a). We use seasonal donor records and do not estimate how that missing-data treatment affects future published SPM thresholds.

Let \(r\) be an annual log growth rate for real expenditures. A donor record from year \(d\) projected to year \(t\) receives the multiplier \((P_t/P_d)\exp[r(t-d)]\). We report two scenarios: zero real growth and a CE trend. To estimate the latter, we construct five nonoverlapping annual collection blocks ending in 2021–2025, express them in common origin-year prices with fixed origin-window component weights, and compute each block’s survey-weighted mean in the 47th–53rd percentile FCSUti band. We regress the log means on year by ordinary least squares to estimate a slope, and we apply half that estimate. We chose the 0.5 shrinkage independently of the reported forecast scores.

We express growth rates as compounded annual equivalents, \(\exp(r)-1\). The expenditure multiplier uses the log rate \(r\). We apply annual real growth of 0.914 percent, from an applied log rate of 0.910 percent. Using ten annual blocks, we obtain 0.817 percent; excluding the two blocks ending in 2021 and 2022 from the five-block fit leaves three blocks and gives −0.135 percent. Ordinary regression uncertainty omits repeated interviews, survey design, serial dependence, and future structural changes. Neither these sensitivities nor the zero-real scenario bound forecast uncertainty.

We use annual CPI-U growth from the Congressional Budget Office (CBO)’s February 2026 economic projections and rebase the nominal path to the observed 2025 annual index (Congressional Budget Office 2026). We assume that every expenditure price component and rents grow at CBO’s projected aggregate rate. Real growth and prices both affect projected spending; setting real growth to zero still allows changes from replacing older observations in the rolling window.

We anchor each tenure’s national threshold and housing share to published 2025 values. Let \(\widetilde a_{T,h}\) denote the housing share that we estimate from the projected microdata for target year \(T\) and tenure \(h\). For origin \(O=2025\), we calculate

\[ \widehat B_{T,h}=B_{O,h}^{\mathrm{published}} \frac{\widetilde B_{T,h}}{\widetilde B_{O,h}},\qquad \widehat a_{T,h}=a_{O,h}^{\mathrm{published}} \frac{\widetilde a_{T,h}}{\widetilde a_{O,h}}. \]

We estimate subsequent changes from the microdata and select the percentile band again for every target window. Changes in band membership can make the tenure-specific threshold under the positive-real-growth scenario slightly lower than under zero real growth in a particular year; the scenarios do not preserve a fixed ranking of selected observations.

7.2 Projecting local relative housing costs

For threshold year \(T\), we use ACS years \(T-5\) through \(T-1\). We allocate each Public Use Microdata Area (PUMA) fractionally to SPM estimation areas using a geographic crosswalk. We use the February 2013 metropolitan delineation and record the crosswalk and source versions. Fractional allocation, public-data topcoding, and the public rental sample limit exact reproduction of the Census calculation (U.S. Census Bureau 2025a).

We select occupied, cash-rented two-bedroom housing units with complete kitchen and plumbing facilities, positive gross rent, and positive housing weights (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-b). For each complete five-year Public Use Microdata Sample (PUMS) product, we retain housing weights (WGTP) and group records by survey year using their identifiers. We do not combine overlapping products as independent observations. We multiply monthly gross rent (GRNTP) once by the housing-dollar adjustment (ADJHSG), divided by \(10^6\), to express rent on the product’s common dollar basis (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-a). We multiply housing weights by PUMA-to-area allocation fractions, sort the adjusted rents, and select the first value whose cumulative weight reaches 50 percent. We do not interpolate between values. For future donor copies, we retain the original product weights without annual reweighting or a population-growth adjustment.

We use observed ACS cohorts through 2024. For each later cohort, we copy the 2024 donor records and scale rents to a common price basis. We recompute local and national weighted rent medians from each complete five-cohort window. Equal nationwide rent growth therefore does not imply constant local relative costs: the mix changes as older observed cohorts leave the window. Once all five cohorts use the same donor distribution, the relative indices stabilize under this assumption. In this snapshot that occurs in 2029. The whole-threshold factor can continue changing as the housing share changes.

The public-data products used for the 2024 anchor and subsequent windows differ. To avoid treating a product change as rent growth, we bridge them over the common 2020–2023 years. If \(m^{v}_{W,j}\) is area \(j\)’s local-to-national rent-median ratio for window \(W\) in product \(v\), then, for an area with a published 2024 anchor,

\[ \widehat g_{T,j}=g^{\mathrm{published}}_{2024,j} \frac{m^{\mathrm{old}}_{2020:2023,j}}{m^{\mathrm{old}}_{2019:2023,j}} \frac{m^{\mathrm{new}}_{T-5:T-1,j}}{m^{\mathrm{new}}_{2020:2023,j}}. \]

Unpublished residual groups retain explicitly modeled, unanchored indices. We estimate indices for 342 published and seven modeled areas in 2022, and 341 published and eight modeled areas from 2023. Annual comparisons distinguish published-index revisions and assignment changes from projected rent growth. In particular, the Massachusetts nonmetropolitan published index changes between 2022 and 2023, and Sumter County moves from a published residual group to an unanchored modeled group; we preserve both discontinuities separately from forecast rent growth.

The 2023-origin, 2024-target geographic comparison covers 341 areas with published anchors. This retrospective comparison excludes the unanchored residual groups and does not test future rent assumptions. Support narrows as observed cohorts leave: 31 areas carry thin-donor warnings in projected years. Preserving record identity avoids mistaking five projected copies for five independent samples. We retain each area’s calculated factor when support is thin and flag its donor count.

We collapse repeated copies to their original donor identities before calculating support. For allocation-expected support, we sum each donor’s geographic allocation fraction once. For donor-collapsed Kish support, we calculate \((\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2\) from each original donor’s total allocated weight \(w_i\). We flag thin support when any of the unique-donor count, allocation-expected count, or Kish count falls below 30. These counts describe donor coverage and unequal weights; they do not estimate survey-design uncertainty.

8 Discussion

We approximate published threshold levels from public CE data. We estimate that adding home internet raises our levels by 0.3 to 4.0 percent and that imputing in-kind benefits raises them by a further 0.5 to 3.5 percent; shelter definitions also move them (Section 4). Sample treatment and price adjustment can affect the estimates; we do not isolate their contributions to the observed discrepancies. Stable proportional differences cancel in adjacent-year growth ratios. We evaluate those growth ratios separately from threshold levels.

In the periods examined, consumption-based growth improves on CPI-U adjustment as a predictor of published SPM thresholds. Annual re-estimation reflects consumption changes that price adjustment alone omits, consistent with the measure’s quasi-relative construction. From 2022 to 2025, BLS’s FCSUti index supplies 33 to 39 percent of its thresholds’ growth beyond CPI-U. Its imputed benefits supply 4.9 to 12.9 percent when each carries its part of the Other share, and 3.5 to 11.8 percent by the share rows BLS publishes for them, with the Other share kept separate. Out-of-pocket spending supplies the rest, with the Other share in the second case; we do not split real spending from sample composition within that part (Section 4). With the CE’s spending summaries, we estimate that adding home internet changes our 2022–2025 growth by −0.54 to +4.33 points. We estimate that imputing in-kind benefits then changes it by −1.62 to −0.19 points, each change within one standard error of zero. Price-indexed anchored measures serve a different purpose: they hold the real standard fixed (Wimer et al. 2016; Creamer 2026b).

We evaluate prospective performance against one year’s outcome. We run the six-year comparison on revised data after BLS published all six outcomes and use a five-year benchmark for the 2025 nowcast. We reuse CE observations across overlapping windows and housing-tenure estimates. These dependent comparisons cannot establish a general method ranking or estimate combination weights.

When BLS releases the complete CE window before publishing thresholds, we can estimate a nowcast from observed consumption. For later years, we project both survey windows under assumptions about consumption and prices. We have not evaluated those longer-horizon projections prospectively. Changes to BLS methods, benefit imputations, and sample composition can alter growth relationships. We test sample and trend assumptions without constructing forecast intervals.

Threshold choice affects modeled poverty through the distribution of resources near the line. A poverty-rate impact estimate therefore requires a specified population and resource model.

The replication used for the 2025 nowcast has mean absolute deviations across tenures of 1.5 to 2.1 percent per year against revised 2019–2024 thresholds, and 1.0 to 1.6 percent against the preceding published series with its corresponding anchor share. Its base case reproduces the imputation receipt’s 2022 rental assistance per assisted renter.

The input manifests record exact source and release identities. The data provenance and forecast record identify the inputs and evidence of the earlier experiments, and data/inkind/provenance.json those of the in-kind experiment.

To verify the reported tables, clone the paper repository with its full tag history and check out the manuscript revision being evaluated. Run python3 -B scripts/check_paper.py and python3 -B -m unittest discover -s tests -v. Quarto 1.9.36 and TeX Live 2026 render HTML and PDF with quarto render --to html and quarto render --to pdf. These commands regenerate the tables from the recorded artifacts; regenerating the underlying CE and ACS estimates additionally requires the pinned calculator and its source files. The experiment replay guide provides commands and source-file requirements for raw-data replication.

The standalone calculator provides a portable projection artifact containing year-specific national bases, housing shares, geographic indices, assignments, and source fingerprints. It includes adapters for PolicyEngine’s tax-benefit model, Microcosm’s population-data tools, and Axiom’s rules engine. The PolicyEngine and Microcosm adapters apply the same forecast to supplied SPM membership, composition, and geography. The Axiom adapter exports forecast inputs to RuleSpec, Axiom’s policy specification format, and invokes the Axiom core runtime to calculate thresholds. Population models supply resources and weights separately. Researchers can reuse the expenditure forecast across engines; each adopting model requires its own runtime and release verification.

In the linked resource implementation, PolicyEngine-US sums modeled housing awards within each household and allocates the total to SPM units by member share, following Census’s subsidy proration (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-b, 14). It also sums tenant payments from units with positive awards and allocates those payments by the same shares. It caps each unit’s SPM housing resource at the housing portion of its threshold less allocated tenant payments, with a minimum of zero. The model calculates eligibility and program awards under U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) programs separately.

Under that resource specification, population-data producers use the householder’s SPM unit to represent an assisted family and treat interview-time housing receipt as full-year receipt. They represent reported public housing and reduced rent through the HUD-family calculation, including reduced-rent programs that may fall outside HUD. The specification assumes the multi-unit tenant-payment split; Census does not document it. These resource conventions do not alter the threshold estimates.

The build verifies artifact hashes, regenerates tables in temporary storage, and compares the outputs with the saved tables and evaluation JSON. It also checks the forecast record and archived evaluation tables.
